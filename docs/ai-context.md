📘 1. Understanding the Governing Documents

Oak Hill Settlement is governed by three primary documents:
	1.	Declaration of Covenants, Conditions & Restrictions (CCRs)
	2.	Bylaws
	3.	Architectural Standards (Board-created guidelines)

Important distinction:

The CCR Declaration is the highest authority.
Board-created “Declarations” or “Resolutions” only hold legal power if:
	•	They are authorized by the CCRs and
	•	Adopted in accordance with ORS 94 (Oregon Planned Community Act)

Many homeowners don’t realize that not everything the Board publishes is actually enforceable.

⚖️ 2. The VF Law Federal Case (Historical Context)

VF Law (formerly Vial Fotheringham), the firm the HOA uses today, has been collecting from Oak Hill homeowners since at least 2009. One of those homeowners took it to federal court: Porras v. Vial Fotheringham LLP, No. 3:13-cv-00699-BR (D. Or., Opinion and Order, May 2015).

What the opinion records:
	•	In December 2010, VF Law sued an Oak Hill homeowner in Washington County Circuit Court over unpaid assessments. He didn't respond and lost by default in May 2011, but the court cut VF Law's requested attorney fees from $1,281.48 to $750.
	•	The homeowner then sued VF Law under the federal Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA).
	•	The federal court held that HOA assessments, and the late fees, attorney fees, and collection costs VF Law adds to them, are "debts" under the FDCPA. Federal debt-collection rules apply when VF Law collects from Oak Hill owners.
	•	The court noted there was no evidence in the record that homeowners were given notice of the HOA's 2009 Collection Resolution.

What the case does not say:
	•	It did not rule that Oak Hill's CC&Rs lack a fining mechanism.
	•	The 2015 opinion did not find that VF Law violated the FDCPA.
	•	It did not address fines for rule or architectural violations.

(Earlier versions of this guide cited "Mendoza v. Columbia Collectors/ VF Law, D. Or., 2011." No such case could be found in public court records, and it should not be cited.)

In 2012 the Board adopted a new Collection Resolution and the Rules Enforcement and Fine Structure Resolution #2012-001. Both are on the Documents page. #2012-001 is a Board resolution, not a CC&R amendment:
	•	CC&R §4.25 allows fines only after reasonable notice and a reasonable opportunity for a hearing
	•	Bylaws §4.15(f) lets the Board adopt enforcement policies by resolution, without an owner vote
	•	ORS 94.630(1)(n) requires a fine to be based on a schedule or resolution delivered to each lot, so owners can ask for proof of delivery
	•	Bylaws §4.7(l) lets owners overrule a Board rule with 75% of the votes at a properly noticed meeting

The case remains relevant because VF Law still represents the HOA, federal debt-collection law applies to what it collects, and the HOA's notice and procedure obligations are where enforcement most often goes wrong.

🛡 3. What the Board Can — and Cannot — Do Under Oregon Law

✔️ The Board can:
	•	Enforce specific, objective CCR requirements
	•	Require ARC approval only for improvements that meet the CCR definition
	•	Maintain common areas
	•	Adopt rules and guidelines within CCR-authorized scope
	•	Levy annual assessments approved by the budget process

❌ The Board cannot:
	•	Invent new fines or penalties not present in the CCRs
	•	Withhold appeal rights (ORS 94.630)
	•	Ignore procedural deadlines
	•	Apply rules inconsistently or discriminatorily
	•	Enforce “guidelines” as if they were CCRs
	•	Treat advisory design preferences as mandatory
	•	Issue legally binding decisions through email without proper notice or meeting procedure

📬 4. History of Procedural Defects in Notices

Homeowners across Oak Hill Settlement have reported serious defects in violation notices, including:

● Missing or incorrect citations

Notices frequently cite:
	•	Clauses that do not apply
	•	Clauses that do not exist
	•	Clauses misinterpreted contrary to plain meaning

● Failure to mention appeal rights

Bylaws §4.15(a)(iii) requires that enforcement notices remind homeowners of their right to a hearing and appeal.

Many notices—including architectural denials and paint compliance notices—omitted this mandatory language, rendering them procedurally defective.

● ARC timelines not followed

Under CCR §6.5, the ARC must:
	•	Issue a written decision within 20 working days
	•	If no written decision is issued within 60 days, the request is automatically approved

Several homeowners have experienced:
	•	ARC decisions made far outside this window
	•	ARC denials referencing reasons not contained in the CCRs
	•	No ARC response at all

● Inaccurate descriptions of property conditions

Examples include:
	•	Notices describing incorrect locations
	•	Notices referencing conditions that did not exist
	•	Paint notices sent broadly to homeowners who had freshly maintained exteriors
	•	Notices instructing homeowners to take actions not found anywhere in the CCRs

These patterns signal systemic issues—both at the Board level and the property management level.

🏚 5. The Procedurally Defective Paint Notices (Recent Example)

In 2025, a series of “courtesy” paint-related notices were distributed across the Settlement. Defects included:
	•	Failure to reference any applicable CCR clause
	•	Misstatements implying ARC approval was required for basic routine maintenance
	•	Inconsistent descriptions of required work
	•	Instructions requiring “licensed and insured painters,” which does not appear in any governing document
	•	No mention of homeowner appeal rights
	•	No identification of what specific “unsightly condition” allegedly violated §4.20 (the only applicable clause)

Why this matters:

CCRs do not require ARC approval for routine maintenance.
They only govern:
	•	Alterations
	•	New construction
	•	Changes in materials, colors, or design

Routine touch-up painting is explicitly within homeowner discretion.

These notices demonstrate why homeowners benefit from independent access to accurate governing document summaries.

🏛 6. Appeal Hearing Requirements (What Must Happen Legally)

Under CCR §6.5 and Bylaws §4.15:
	•	Homeowners have the right to a fair hearing
	•	Hearings must be held within the CCR-specified timeline
	•	The homeowner must receive proper notice
	•	The Board must base decisions on the CCRs, not hypothetical concerns
	•	The Board must issue decisions in writing
	•	Decisions must reference actual governing documents, not personal preference or hypothetical fears

Documented procedural failures have included:
	•	Hearings held outside the required timeframe
	•	Failure to acknowledge material submitted by homeowners
	•	Failure to cite any applicable CCR clause
	•	Decisions based on “hypothetical concerns” rather than rules
	•	Property manager providing contradictory or inaccurate information to homeowners

When these procedural steps are not followed, the resulting enforcement action is legally defective.

🏠 7. Why Understanding These Patterns Matters for Homeowners

Many homeowners feel intimidated by HOA notices.
This guide is meant to empower you.

You have rights:
	•	You have the right to timely, accurate, procedurally compliant enforcement
	•	You have the right to an appeal
	•	You have the right to be heard
	•	You have the right to access governing documents
	•	You have the right to challenge defective notices
	•	You have the right to organize with neighbors

You also have the right to remove Board members.

Under ORS 94.640(6), ORS 94.650, and our Bylaws (194 lots, one vote each):
	•	Owners holding 30% of the votes (59 lots) can require a special meeting (Bylaws §3.4)
	•	If the Board doesn’t give notice within 30 days, an owner who signed can call the meeting (ORS 94.650(3))
	•	Quorum is 20% (39 lots), in person or by proxy (Bylaws §3.6)
	•	Removal takes a majority of all votes, 98 of 194, cast in person, by absentee ballot, or by proxy (Bylaws §3.10, §4.6)
	•	Removal must be on the meeting notice, and each director gets a chance to be heard and a separate vote (ORS 94.640(6)(b))
	•	A removed director serves until owners elect a successor (ORS 94.640(6)(c), (7))

This is a check-and-balance that exists precisely to prevent abuse or misunderstanding of HOA authority.

👥 9. Patterns of Board and Management Overreach

Without referencing any private homeowner situation, here are the general recurring patterns observed in Oak Hill Settlement:
	•	Expansion of ARC authority beyond what CCRs permit
	•	Treating guidelines as if they were binding law
	•	Failing to follow required notice or appeal timelines
	•	Citing incorrect CCR clauses
	•	Fine threats that don’t state the fine or show the resolution was delivered to the lot
	•	Using personal opinions (“we don’t want this in the community”) instead of CCR text
	•	Issuing enforcement based on hypothetical future scenarios
	•	Failure to properly document Board decisions
	•	Failure to respond to homeowner submissions (including certified letters)
	•	Unclear or conflicting violation notices

Patterns like these are why independent homeowner education matters.
